PantryPal

an AI cooking partner

App Store (opens in a new tab) Try it here

PantryPal answers one question: what can I cook with what I already have? Scan a receipt or the fridge and it stocks a shelf. Then, instead of a menu, dinner is already on the screen: one dish, ranked against that shelf.

I took it from sketches to the App Store: the wireframes, the Figma prototype, and then the real thing, a SwiftUI app with its own design system and the AI service behind it.

Build

the Figma file becomes a phone

Then I built it for real, in SwiftUI, with Claude Code as a pair. The design system came first, lifted out of Figma into tokens and components, so every screen after it was built from the same parts, and checked against its Figma frame.

how it looks

one source of truth
  1. Figmasix frames, every colour and size in them
  2. tokensseven JSON files, each value naming the frame it came from
  3. PantryPalDSa Swift package: the tokens and seventeen components
  4. the screensbuilt from it, and diffed against Figma, pixel by pixel

how it thinks

no keys in the app
  1. the phonethe shelf, the chef’s book and every drawing, offline
  2. a proxysmall functions on Vercel that hold the AI keys
  3. Geminireads receipts and fridges, writes dishes, draws food
  4. and backa slow or dead network falls back to the book, never a spinner

Every drawing, one contract

A written style contract turns any food into one of the app’s stickers. Five style tests were judged against the Figma art before the first batch.

Then the fifty most-used foods were drawn in one run: 53 renders in all for $4.59, and one sent back, because the model drew a live sheep for lamb. Food stays food.

Rethink

fourteen pots on one stove

Living with the real app showed what the prototype had hidden: its first screen was a control panel. Before anything delicious happened, it asked you to weigh fourteen things.

the hub, counted

So the app flipped its premise. It opens on an answer, not a menu, and the shelf became a side door: always there, never required.

Details

the small calls that make it feel right

  • Ten dishes, not fifty

    Ranking all fifty is what makes the top of the deck good. Offering all fifty turns a suggestion into a catalogue, and nobody swipes to dish forty.

  • Salt is already in the kitchen

    Seven of the first ten dishes listed salt or oil, so the chef kept sending people shopping for seasoning. The basics now sit outside the score.

  • A chef is just a name

    The email and password guarded nothing the phone’s own lock didn’t. Now one name opens the kitchen, and the profile never leaves the phone.

  • Throw it either way

    The dish card only answered sideways, so a flick up felt stuck. Now it moves whichever way you push it, and snaps back to the top pick whenever the shelf changes.

  • Say what the button does

    “Cook it!” read as final, when the tap only opens the steps: it says Start cooking now. A refresh icon promised new dishes that never came, so Show me another became Next dish.

  • Pay for a drawing once

    Drawings are the app’s real running cost. Every render is kept and harvested back into the app, so each food is drawn once, ever, and then works offline.

Results

what shipped, what I learned

1 tapfrom a stocked shelf to cooking; the prototype asked for a basket, a review and a confirm first

50dishes ranked on the phone itself, with no network and no wait

165drawings bundled in the app, so the book never waits for its art

272tests: 198 unit, and 74 that drive the real app on every size of phone

  1. Let the real thing redesign you. The prototype’s flow looked right until I lived with it; four working prototypes settled what opinions couldn’t.
  2. Count, don’t argue. Taps, screens and waits turned a matter of taste into evidence anyone could check.
  3. Build the system before the screens. With the tokens in place, the redesign changed what the app did without touching how it looked.
  4. What I’d do next: test with cooks I’ve never met. Friendly feedback only gets you so far.
